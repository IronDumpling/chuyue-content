# 1

On the fifth day of my treatment, or rather at the end of my fifth session, a girl from my ward died in her cell.

She died like a bird bathed in blood, poised for flight, straining with everything she had, as if trying to escape the cramped space that held her.

Her body wasn't found until the chief physician led his rounds. Judging by the scene, she had been dead for at least five hours. And all that time, the rest of us in the ward had been in treatment. No one noticed the tragedy quietly unfolding right beside them.

When I was first admitted, my attending physician had asked her to show me around the hospital. She was hardly a competent guide. Every time I trailed after her through the halls, she kept glancing about warily, asking me again and again whether I knew where the windows were. But even I knew there were no windows here. Before I could answer, she would ask whether I had ever seen a fawn by the sea.

The next day, I asked my doctor for a different guide.

Though we had spoken a few times, I never learned her name. In fact, everyone here had forgotten their names. According to my doctor, by the time I could remember my real name, my treatment would be nearly over.

The thing is, I was in no hurry to leave.

Life here was full and good. The hospital was elegantly minimal, the cafeteria food was healthy and delicious, and my doctor was conscientious and kind. Most of all, the treatment was remarkably comfortable, and my memories really were coming back, bit by bit.

The hospital held over a thousand patients spread across more than a hundred wards, seven to a ward. Each ward was a perfect square, its four walls lined with black and white tiles, like a giant Rubik's cube assembled from chessboards. The white tiles gave off a faint glow that lit the seven transparent cells floating in the room until they gleamed like crystal. Each cell was a self-contained living space, furnished with a bed, a desk, cabinets, and everything one needed for daily life.

The cells drifted slowly around the room to simulate real-world acceleration. Strangely, I never saw two of them collide. Then again, maybe I had just never paid attention. After all, for most of the time we spent inside the cells, what we saw was not the real world.

It truly was a marvelous experience. Thinking of it, I couldn't help marveling at how astonishing technological progress could be.

When I first arrived, my mind was completely empty. Apart from knowing I was here to recover my memories, I knew nothing at all about my own life. Looking around that enormous black-and-white room, I was hit by a wave of dizziness. It wasn't until the doctor and a nurse helped me into a hospital gown that I recovered a little strength.

One face of each cell had no wall; that was the entrance. I stood in front of the cell assigned to me, hesitating over whether to step inside. There was no privacy here at all. I couldn't understand how anyone could live in a place like this. Could such an avant-garde, oppressive design really put people at ease enough to accept treatment?

My doctor seemed to read my doubts.

"You don't need to worry about not fitting in here," he said with a smile. "This place was designed to help you fit in."

I still didn't understand what he meant.

"Don't rush." He waved a hand at the wall of the cell, and a pale blue control panel immediately appeared.

He told me to step inside. I did.

"One session lasts twelve hours, and there's no limit on the number of sessions. Statistically, most people need about thirty." As he spoke, he pressed the confirm button on the panel.

A transparent glass door instantly slid down over the entrance. Then the cell's twelve edges lit up one after another, and a soft white light filled the space. It wasn't harsh at all; if anything, it had a reassuring warmth. The cell rose slowly and hovered in midair. At the same time, the clear glass gradually frosted over, cutting me off completely from the world outside.

I started to panic.

"It won't fall, will it?"

"No. It runs on a precise, stable electromagnetic track, designed specifically for treatment."

I heard the doctor's voice drift up from below, but all I could see was his blurry figure shrinking away.

"For treatment?"

"Yes. You'll understand in a moment." His voice grew more and more distant. "Good luck."

I felt gravity slowly fading away. Or rather, some force was canceling it out. Little by little, I lost all my weight and floated in the center of the cell.

Before I could get used to the sensation, a black hexagon suddenly appeared before my eyes, as if someone had slit the pure white space open with a knife. New hexagons kept replicating from its edges, devouring the white around them cell by cell. By the time the last bit of light vanished, I could no longer tell where I was.

I looked around and noticed a tiny point of light at the far end of the horizon. I reached out to touch it.

At that moment, a fierce wind came roaring in. I raised my hands to shield my face. The wind howled past my ears, laced with faint, chaotic electronic noise. The whole world seemed to be blown away into the distance, yet the point of light only grew brighter.

The next instant, there was nothing before me but a vast, blank whiteness.

After a brief blindness, a scene gradually emerged. Like a drop of black ink falling onto snow-white rice paper, the world before me spread outward from a single point, slowly revealing a long street.

At first, the colors and shapes of the street kept shifting. Buildings twisted, then melted; the texture of the pavement flickered like data that hadn't finished loading.

After a while, everything finally settled.

Sight came back first, then taste and hearing, and touch came last. The wind brushed my cheeks, the flowers and grass by the road gave off a fresh fragrance, and the feeling of my clothes against my skin grew clearer. Everything before me became more and more real, so real that I forgot what I had just been through.

I began walking down the street. The street gave way to a valley, the valley to a desert, the desert to a beach. The scenes changed as fast as a carelessly edited montage. They had nothing to do with each other, yet somehow they joined together seamlessly, so much so that I couldn't tell what was unreal. It was as if the world was simply meant to be this way.

At last, I sat down on a black-sand beach, closed my eyes, and listened quietly to the waves.

Suddenly I wondered: once I closed my eyes, did the world still go on being there?

A lilting birdsong interrupted my thoughts.

I opened my eyes and saw a small grayish-brown bird perched on a rock not far away. It looked utterly unremarkable, yet its song was astonishingly clear, one call after another, as if it were calling out for something.

It was a nightingale.

Suddenly, my doctor's voice came from the edge of the sky.

"How are you settling in?"

I nodded. "What is this place?"

"Ha, I'm afraid I can't answer that," he said. "All I can tell you is that it's inside your head."

He then explained the principles of Blue Therapy and the rules to follow during treatment. He was rather vague about it. Combining what he said with my experience over the following days, I summed it all up in six points:

1. Blue Therapy is a form of psychotherapy in which the patient actively participates. Its core consists of the sessions inside the cell and the question-and-answer conversations with the attending physician during treatment.
2. The virtual images inside the cell come from the patient's conscious or subconscious mind. Simply put, the cell is the patient's subconscious space.
3. These images help patients recover their memories, and also help doctors analyze and treat patients' psychological and mental illnesses.
4. Since all patients have amnesia, medical staff may not prompt patients by any means other than the images in the cell.
5. Patients must not remove their hospital gowns during treatment, or the somatosensory system will fail.
6. A single session must never exceed twenty hours, or it may cause permanent trauma.

After hearing all this, I finally understood. The cell was the window through which patients traveled the world.

Over the next few days, I witnessed the storms hidden in the depths of my own subconscious.

Purple lightning split the night sky and struck an endless sheet of ice. A blue whale so vast I could see neither its head nor its tail burst up through the ice, raising a cloud of snow that blotted out the sky.

Missiles screamed through plasma clouds, their explosions loud enough, it seemed, to level a city.

Countless giant hands descended from the sky, reaching for the ground, grabbing and grabbing until they tore the earth out by its roots and let molten lava swallow everything.

I never knew what these visions had to do with my life. Strangely, at the end of every session, I recovered another small piece of the puzzle of my blank life. In each conversation, my doctor recorded everything I remembered, compiled it into a booklet, and gave it to me to read.

The first thing to come back was a tall iron gate, its paint peeling in patches. I stood at the gate holding a stuffed fawn, my fingers red with cold.

"Who were you waiting for?" my doctor asked.

I shook my head. All I remembered was that I had waited a very long time.

In later sessions, a streetlamp appeared beside the gate, and beneath it stood two blurry figures. Later still, the figures gained faces and voices. They bent down and took my hands. At last I remembered: they were my parents.

"Your parents loved you very much, didn't they?"

I nodded, my eyes growing warm. I remembered the warmth of their palms, the street bathed in golden sunlight, the stuffed fawn in my arms.

Another scene came back the same way, a little at a time.

At first there was only an afternoon. The sky was bright, almost painfully bright. I wore a long white dress, and something cold was slipped onto my finger. Everyone around me had their heads tilted back, staring at the sky, while windowpanes in the distance hummed and rattled. I wanted to look up too, but my neck felt as if something were holding it down.

"What were they looking at?"

"I don't know."

"Then let's not think about it for now," my doctor said gently. "Take your time."

Each time after that, the sky was a little softer than the time before. Sunlight fell on the lawn, the wind lifted the hem of my dress, and someone held my hand. Fewer and fewer people were looking up, until finally they all turned around and smiled at me.

"So few people. Don't you find that a little sad?" my doctor asked, leafing through his notes.

"No." I thought about it. "I suppose I just don't like crowds."

He smiled and wrote a few lines in the record.

Meanwhile, some familiar yet strange words began surfacing in my mind.

State.

Citizen.

People.

I knew their most basic meanings, but I couldn't grasp what they actually meant in society today.

"Understands the basic definitions, but not the meanings modern society has given them..." My doctor flipped through his notes. "Hmm. That's a new finding."

Although I had forgotten so much, I didn't feel any regret. Getting my memories back felt wonderful. The fragments that had once made up my life were like shards of glass scattered in the dark. I didn't know how much I had lost, but each time I found a piece, I could see a little of the light that had always been mine.

I read those records over and over. The earliest pages always felt a bit foreign, like sentences written by someone else. Maybe back then I hadn't truly woken up yet. I longed for the day I would remember my own name. Thinking about it, I often found myself in tears.

And so, right up until the end of my fifth session, everything went smoothly.

Then the girl died.

# 2

As soon as the body was found, the nurses and doctors sealed off the ward. With practiced efficiency, they cleared the scene, removed the body, and scrubbed the floor, as though it were a drill they had rehearsed countless times.

The chief physician solemnly informed everyone in the ward that there was no official conclusion yet as to the cause of death, that no one was to spread unverified information, and that he hoped we would all understand.

Naturally, I had no intention of stirring up trouble, but the others might. In the end, word got out.

A patient's unexpected death is no small matter. Within days, the whole hospital was on edge. Many people no longer wanted to continue treatment. Some applied for transfers; some even demanded their sessions be stopped immediately.

My doctor told me this was the first death he had encountered since he started working here. As for the cause, not only were the patients full of theories, even the doctors couldn't agree among themselves. So it would likely remain a mystery.

I asked him what would happen to the people who wanted to leave.

"As far as I know, as long as their families agree, most patients will continue treatment under the agreements they signed."

"And the rest?"

He glanced at me.

"That small minority will regret leaving."

His tone was flat. For a moment I didn't know what to say.

Then, to my surprise, he burst out laughing.

"Hahaha, spend all that money and walk out without being cured? Of course they'll regret it."

Only then did I realize I didn't even know how much the treatment cost, or whether my family could afford it.

"But you don't need to worry," he went on. "Your husband is very good to you. He fully supports your treatment."

Then he stopped short.

"Sorry. I shouldn't have said that."

Husband? So I was married.

I thought of that sunny afternoon, the long white dress, the cold thing on my finger, and the hand holding mine. So that had been my wedding.

Instinctively, I looked down at my fingers. There was nothing on them.

Before I could ask anything more, he changed the subject.

"Oh, right. A new patient is joining your ward today. Remember to show her around."

The new patient was number C02, a young woman. She had long black hair and bright eyes, and there was a sharpness in her gaze that set her apart from everyone around her. By any measure, she didn't look like someone with a mental illness.

Still, her first session was dramatic. Screams and error alarms rang out one after another, and in the end, the medical staff had to drag her out of her cell. I had just finished my sixth session and saw the whole thing.

Once she was a little better, I sat down beside her in the cafeteria to carry out the task my doctor had given me.

"Hi. I'm C04."

"C04..." she repeated under her breath. She seemed not to have recovered from her first session yet. Her eyes were bloodshot, and the veins at her temples kept twitching.

"Not used to it yet?" I smiled. "Don't worry. A few more sessions and you'll get used to it." The moment the words left my mouth, I realized my own first time had been nowhere near as rough as hers.

She was silent for a long while before she looked up.

"Aren't you... afraid?"

"Of what?"

"Of the possibility that..." She hesitated. "I mean, what if that world is the real one?"

I was taken aback. So her clever appearance was just a front. Sure enough, anyone who ended up here had to have something wrong with them.

"How could it be? That's your subconscious. It changes with your thoughts. It doesn't follow common sense to begin with..."

"Do you know what common sense is?"

The question stopped me.

"Common sense is... the things everyone knows."

"But we don't even remember the world we used to live in. How would we know what common sense is?"

I wanted to argue, but it suddenly struck me that all my judgments had been based on the world the hospital presented to me. In truth, I had no real evidence that this place was reality.

She looked around and started searching for windows again.

"Why are there no windows here?"

I suddenly thought of the girl who had died. The same gestures, the same expression. For an instant, I had the illusion that the two of them were somehow connected.

"Because they don't want us to see outside," C02 answered her own question. "They're hiding something."

Over the next several lunches, she shared all kinds of outlandish theories with me.

Sometimes she suspected this place was conducting human experiments, and that patients who were "cured" weren't sent home at all but shipped off to factories to be dismantled. Other times she thought we weren't human at all, but artificial intelligences made by the hospital, and that the researchers kept refining our models until we had minds exactly like real humans.

Once, she even picked up a table knife and tried to cut open her forearm to see whether blood or engine oil would come out. Luckily, I stopped her in time.

"I finally understand why you ended up in the hospital."

"Because you think I'm crazy?"

"So you know."

"I'm not crazy." She put down the knife. "I've just reflected on a few more things than you have."

"Reflected on what?"

"On the things all of you take for granted."

I shook my head, unwilling to argue with her any further.

"So, any new theories today?"

"Actually, I've thought it over and over, and I think my theory from the first day might be right after all."

"How so?"

"Let me ask you. How do you tell dreams from reality?"

"Reality is more normal, and you're more lucid..."

"See? You don't reflect."

She gave a smug little smile.

"In a dream, you usually can't remember yourself in reality, but in reality you can remember yourself in the dream. So in reality, you can find the boundary between dream and waking, and you feel lucid, while the dream version of you is forced to accept a... script."

I had to admit it made sense.

"But now, when we enter that so-called subconscious world, we can still remember our lives in the hospital."

"And," I picked up where she left off, "here in the hospital, we can also remember what happened in the subconscious world."

"Exactly," she said. "If both worlds can remember each other, then what's the difference between them?"

"So how do you tell them apart?"

"I don't know."

She rested her chin in one hand and idly twirled her utensils.

"Dreams can't explain it, and common sense can't settle it. Worse, we don't even know how much time we've actually lived through." She paused. "So I'm still thinking."

Though I still refused to accept her absurd claims, the girl's unexplained death, the permanently sealed hospital, and this peculiar form of treatment meant I could no longer feel as at ease as before.

I began to doubt. I just wasn't ready to admit that I was doubting.

It was in those same few days that I remembered something else.

I was lying in a white hospital bed, my belly hollow. My husband sat at the bedside, gripping my hand tightly. The curtains were half drawn, and the room was very quiet.

"Were you sad at the time?" my doctor asked.

"No," I said. "We talked it over many times. We both agreed it was the best decision."

The words came out almost on their own, smooth and composed, like an answer prepared long in advance. As soon as I said it, I froze.

Was I really not sad?

I tried to go back to that hospital room, to see my husband's expression clearly, but his face stayed blurred. All I remembered was how tightly he held my hand, so tightly it hurt. There seemed to be someone else in the room too, silently watching us the whole time, but I couldn't remember what that person looked like.

The harder I tried to remember, the quieter the image became, as quiet as a photograph someone had carefully wiped clean.

"C04?"

I came back to myself and found tears had fallen onto the back of my hand. But I couldn't say why I was crying.

My doctor put down his pen and looked at me for a long time.

"C04, you seem a little emotionally unstable." He closed the record book. "Should we postpone your thirteenth session today?"

I shook my head. Even if there was something wrong with the treatment, I wanted to recover my past. That was why I had come here, and I didn't want to give up halfway.

And so, as the cell slowly rose, the ink-wash scroll unrolled before my eyes once more.

I was back on the black-sand beach. Waves washed over the shore again and again, leaving damp, dark marks behind.

The rock was empty. The nightingale hadn't appeared in a long time.

A rapid buzzing came from behind me.

I turned and saw a small, dark red bird hovering in midair.

It seemed to be a hummingbird, but not quite like the hummingbirds I remembered. Its body was astonishingly small, its wings beating so fast they were nearly invisible, and its dark red feathers caught the light with an almost metallic sheen.

It circled me a few times, then flew straight toward a distant hill and landed on the summit.

Curious, I followed. Just as I got close, it flew off somewhere even farther. After this happened several times, I finally realized it seemed to want me to follow it.

Was my subconscious guiding me toward something?

I followed the hummingbird along rugged mountain paths, across vast plains, and through a raging river. I don't know how long I walked before I finally arrived at the foot of a mountain. There was a cave there, glowing with a faint blue light. From deep within came the soft sound of running water.

I decided to follow the sound inside, but the hummingbird vanished at that moment.

The cave was studded with blue crystals, their faint glow lighting only a small patch of ground at my feet. I had to steady myself against the rock wall and inch forward.

The dripping of water came and went, sometimes clear, sometimes muffled. Whenever it suddenly grew louder, I froze in fright and listened hard for any other sound mixed in. Footsteps? Breathing? Something else? Whatever it was, I was fairly sure it was following me.

Whenever I stopped, the sound stopped too. I spun around, but saw nothing. Whatever it was, it was alert, always darting away the instant I turned my head.

I kept walking, no longer looking back, and instead watched the reflections in the crystals on the rock wall. In their faint refractions, I finally made out what was following me: it seemed to be a fawn.

I pretended not to have noticed and kept going, waiting for the right moment to catch it. Gradually the faint blue glow became light, the light became beams, and the beams converged into a blue ocean at the end of the cave: a pale blue wall of light, like water, like ice.

As I stared at the wall in a daze, I realized I could see the fawn behind me clearly in its reflection. So I found the right angle, backed slowly toward it, then spun around and grabbed it.

It didn't try to escape. Instead it nuzzled my hand, as if glad I had finally caught it. Then it lowered its head and calmly tore open its own belly. There was no blood inside, only a gleaming, crystalline bone.

I was stunned by what I saw. The fawn let out a few cries and pointed at the bone with its nose, as if it wanted me to take it out.

I took it out and held it up to the blue wall of light. Under the pale blue glow, the bone shone with endless fractal light, as if it held an entire galaxy.

I felt that the random angles inside the bone might form something, so I kept turning it. Before long, three characters faintly appeared within.

It was...

In an instant, it was as if hundreds of keys turned in their locks at once, and countless chains snapped.

Emotions, images, words, and sounds came flooding out all at once. They didn't feel like memories. They felt like countless sharp knives, all stabbing into my brain together. The sheer volume of information nearly tore every nerve apart.

Alarms blared. Red and yellow warning windows popped up one after another, piling over each other until they covered the entire image of the cave. Then, with a thunderous roar, the blue wall of light collapsed. All those images, words, and sounds vanished in an instant along with it.

I jolted awake.

Glaring light shone straight into my eyes. I instinctively raised a hand to block it, only to find my body bound by countless cables. I couldn't move. Several people gathered around, untangled the lines on my body, and helped me lie back down.

Where was I? A moment ago I had been in the cave.

What had I seen? What were those three characters?

I tried hard to remember, but all that remained in my mind was a blurred image. Something seemed to have been flying toward me, and then the roar of an explosion swallowed everything.

Hurried footsteps approached. My doctor had arrived. He put on gloves, shone a penlight into my right eye, and said gravely, "C04, your condition is very concerning. I said from the start we shouldn't have rushed into treatment."

It took a long time before I could see everything clearly. I was lying on an enormous, semi-enclosed hospital bed. Several nurses pushed a medicine cart out of the room, while my doctor sat at the bedside checking the readings on various instruments.

"You were rushed into the ICU," he said. "Any later and we couldn't have saved you."

I wanted to ask him why, but no sound came from my throat.

"Your session was nearly twenty hours long! Why didn't you hear the time-limit alarm? What on earth did you run into in your subconscious?"

"A bird..."

Something caught in my throat and I broke into a violent coughing fit. It was a while before I could go on. "There was a..."

I stopped abruptly. How could the doctor not know what I had seen in the cell? If all of it was computed by a machine, then he should know exactly what I'd been through.

"C04, what's wrong?"

I didn't answer.

I remembered the hummingbird leading me in search of something. I seemed to have remembered something because of it, but then the treatment was suddenly cut off, and the doctor knew nothing about it.

As I thought and remembered, the beeping of the instruments gradually quickened.

The doctor didn't press me further. Instead, he called for a nurse to prepare some kind of injection.

I looked around in silence and saw that I was in a sealed ward. A nurse swiped open the door with the access card on her chest and walked toward my bed carrying a steel tray full of medicine and syringes.

Fear crept into me, little by little. Maybe I had accidentally remembered something I wasn't supposed to.

"This isn't the hospital." I stared at my doctor. "What is this place?"

"What are you talking about? Of course this is the hospital."

"Then why is there a window?"

He paused and turned to look at the wall, as if to make sure what I was talking about.

In that instant, I tore the cables off my body, sat up, and shoved him to the floor. I jumped off the bed, but my legs were too weak, and I fell hard at the nurse's feet. She cried out and lost her balance. The tray flew out of her hands, scattering vials and instruments across the floor.

Ignoring the pain, I scrambled up, snatched the access card from her hand, and ran out of the ICU.

"C04 has escaped! Emergency, requesting backup!" my doctor's hoarse shout came from behind me.

Red alarms lit up one after another along the corridor. I ran as hard as I could, trying to avoid the cameras and the staff coming and going. My body was so weak it could barely hold me up, but I was growing more and more certain that this was not the hospital where I had been living. At least, not a place I could normally have reached.

Rapid footsteps sounded behind me. I immediately changed direction, only to find people coming from the other end too. The footsteps grew denser and denser. It sounded like at least twenty people were closing in on me.

I stopped.

"Is this the end...?"

I leaned against the wall and slowly slid down to the floor.

"Then again, maybe it's better not to know."

I lowered my head and buried it in my curled-up body, waiting for someone to take me away.

But they didn't come right away. I lifted my eyes just enough to see the gleaming corridor floor. Suddenly, I noticed a line of text reflected on the floor not far away.

I looked up and saw, beside a tightly closed door, clear as day: **Age-Eligible People's Therapy Center.**

The words were like a decoder constantly adjusting its frequency. The blurred images in my mind began to flicker, bright then dark. As the words came into focus, so did the images. Finally, at a certain moment, the images stopped shaking and became sharp and clear.

*Click.*

I was once abandoned by my parents at the door of the People's Therapy Center, with only a stuffed fawn left beside me. The staff of the People's Welfare Home took me in. Later, they told me my parents believed their upbringing would limit my future, so they had decided to give me up.

I once received distinguished clients on the ground floor of a skyscraper, worn to exhaustion by their unreasonable demands. Every day I checked my performance ranking again and again. Even in my dreams I was filling out forms, just to hit the month's quota and not be replaced by someone else.

I once held my wedding with my husband on a clear afternoon, attended by only a handful of people. Just as I put on my wedding ring, a fleet that blotted out the sky suddenly emerged from the clouds. A barrage like fireworks tore the sky apart and blasted a building that sheltered the Underground into rubble.

I can no longer remember whether the wedding music ever played. But the voice declaring that "State Power must not be defied" lingered in every corner of that day, along with the smell of smoke.

I once argued with my husband all night about whether to keep the child in my belly. The broken fluorescent tube on the floor reflected his haggard face. He buried his face in his hands and wept, telling me he didn't have much time left. He had to stake our whole family's fate on it and give it everything he had.

So the child could not be kept.

I once rode the rail car to and from work every day. In the carriage, propaganda about the nation's achievements played on a loop, the screens scrolling with inspiring news. Late at night, I rode home on a rail car crammed with discount ads.

I often felt an inexplicable pride, and just as often an anxiety I couldn't explain. Through the car window, I could always see the city ablaze with lights. And in the window's reflection, all I could see was a faint flicker of flame, wavering in the wind.

I once watched a coworker, plagued by heart disease and obesity, drop dead while working overtime. Before he died, he kept murmuring over and over: "Must become a Citizen... must become a Citizen..." I knew I couldn't take sick leave. The moment the Citizens learned I was ill, they would immediately find someone healthier to replace me.

I once walked my husband out of the hall where the Citizenship Assessment was held. His face was ashen, his eyes dark, his legs barely able to hold him up. Holding back my own grief, I kept comforting him, telling him he would still have another chance.

But he just kept muttering: "Just two points short."

"Just two points short..."

I once saw off a coworker in the morning as she went, tearful, to her People's psychotherapy session, then ran into her that same evening, all smiles. She took my hand and told me, from the bottom of her heart: "In an instant I realized I'd just been too ungrateful. Why think so much? Life is so beautiful!"

She smiled and patted me on the shoulder.

"You should understand that too."

Then she turned away cheerfully and melted into the crowd.

"C04! Stop resisting, put your hands on your head, and come out slowly!"

My pursuers' voices pulled me back to reality, and time seemed to start flowing again.

In that instant, most of the puzzle of my life had been filled in.

I held my forehead. I wanted to laugh, but couldn't. I wanted to cry, but had no strength left. Getting my memories back felt so bitter. I finally knew what I had lived through, yet I couldn't draw the slightest comfort from it. The life I had forgotten hadn't become any more beautiful for returning to my mind.

All I could do was let out a self-mocking sigh.

Was Blue Therapy meant to make me remember, or to make me forget? Was it protecting me, or destroying me?

I suddenly thought of C02. That half-mad woman, and all her seemingly absurd theories. Could it be that she knew something, and was only pretending to be crazy? And the girl who died, why did she die?

I didn't want to be dragged back like this. There were still so many questions without answers. If I had to lose my future here before I'd even remembered my own name, I couldn't accept it.

I raised the stolen access card and tried to open the door of the People's Therapy Center.

Nothing. I swiped it again. Still nothing. The hospital must have already deactivated the card.

"Final warning! Come out now, or we will use force!"

The footsteps drew closer. With nowhere left to go, I pressed my back against the door and squeezed my eyes shut.

Just then, the lock clicked. The door opened.

I had no time to think. I rushed inside. The door closed again behind me just before my pursuers arrived, shutting them out.

Someone was helping me!

My first thought was of the hummingbird. But could something from the subconscious really interfere with reality?

As the lights in the room came on row by row, I finally saw what lay before me.

Thousands of cylindrical containers stood in neat rows, stretching farther than I could see. Each one was sealed shut; there was no way to tell what was inside.

They moved to a fixed rhythm: every minute, one row of containers would rotate and descend; half a minute later, they would rotate and rise back to their original positions.

In front of each container was a display showing its row and number.

Row A, No. 8 【Injected: 30/30 sets】【Status: Complete】

Row B, No. 8 【Injected: 27/30 sets】【Status: In Progress】

...

When it was Row C's turn to descend, I noticed one container still standing in place.

I ran over at once. The display read, plain as day: **Row C, No. 4 【Injected: 13/30 sets】【Status: Error】**

The container required iris and fingerprint verification. I completed both. With a hiss of steam, it slowly opened. It was empty.

I looked around. There was no other way out. Summoning my courage, I stepped inside, but the container didn't close on its own. I felt around the inner wall, searching for whatever came next.

Suddenly, I felt a bump on the wall behind me. I turned sideways and ran my hand over it. It seemed to be a switch. I tried pressing it, flipping it, but couldn't get it to open.

The familiar buzzing sounded again, and I whipped my head around. Before I could even make out the hummingbird, the floor beneath my feet vanished, and I plunged into a vast space.

It seemed to be a dark red movie theater. All around was silence; only dim light outlined the shapes of the seats and the screen.

But just as I was about to land, the entire theater vanished into thin air, like a projector being switched off.

*Thud.*

I hit the floor of my cell.

I was back?

"C04, how are you feeling? Does anything hurt?" My doctor was looking at me anxiously through the glass. Beside him stood the other patients from my ward.

I looked around. Black and white walls, floating cells, familiar light. This was where I had been living all along!

So everything that just happened had taken place in my subconscious?

"You've been in there for almost twenty-four hours!" There was clear anxiety in the doctor's voice. "It's a miracle you regained consciousness at all!"

The cell door opened, and C02 rushed in at once and threw her arms around me.

"I'm so glad you're okay!"

There were tears in her eyes. I smiled helplessly and reached out to wipe them away. She took my hand and said she could do it herself.

The other patients came over one by one to hug me. Finally, my doctor stepped in front of me.

"C04, your vital signs look normal for now, but to be safe, we'll need to run another checkup." He paused. "After all, someone in this ward has already died."

I nodded. Tension made me clench my fist without thinking, and suddenly I felt something in my palm.

After my doctor left, I opened my hand. It was a folded slip of paper, with just three words written on it: **Don't trust him.**

I looked up at C02. She had already slipped quietly back into the crowd.

I wasn't sure whether the note had come from her, but I no longer believed this hospital was just an ordinary treatment facility.

During the checkup that followed, I dodged as many of my doctor's questions as I could. But so as not to arouse his suspicion, I drew on my earlier memories and told him about the black-sand beach, the blue cave, and so on. He seemed to let his guard down for the time being.

After the checkup, I returned to the ward. Thinking over everything that had happened that day, C02 seemed to be the only one who knew something. I decided to find a quiet spot in the cafeteria and trade what I knew with her, starting with the hidden door I had found.

But no sooner had I sat down beside her with my tray, before I could say a word, than she was called away by the medical staff. She was unusually compliant, head bowed, without a word.

I rushed over and grabbed her. The staff immediately warned me not to interfere with a patient's treatment. I had no choice but to let go.

How could I get a message to her? Countless phrases flashed through my mind, but none was both veiled enough and clear enough.

I'd just have to take a gamble!

I took a deep breath and called out to her: "Little Bird?"

C02 stopped. She turned around and looked me straight in the eye.

"Fawn?"

# 3

This session, I found myself in a forest.

It was the most agonizing wait I could remember. I kept wondering: how had C02 gotten into my subconscious? Why could she guide me to that cave? Was she a madwoman or a genius? A friend or an enemy?

At last, the familiar buzzing came from deep in the woods. The dark red hummingbird appeared before me once again.

Just as I was about to speak, it hovered right at my lips.

I hesitated, then finally realized I couldn't speak freely. So I chattered about nothing in particular while wandering aimlessly through the forest.

Suddenly, the hummingbird settled between my hands. I cupped it, not understanding what it wanted.

"Little Bird, you..."

Before I could finish, my teeth and the tip of my tongue touched something hard.

I froze. The hummingbird's beak was in my mouth!

A tingling current spread from my mouth to my brain, and I lost all ability to move.

What was happening? What was it transmitting to me?

Everything went black.

When my vision returned, C02 was standing in front of me. We were in a pitch-black space. No sky, no ground, nothing to get my bearings by.

"Now we can talk freely," she said with a smile. "This is a zone the outside can't detect. But it'll only hold for half an hour at most."

I still hadn't recovered from the shock.

"Just now, you and I..."

"Just a little kiss for Fawn, that's all."

She said it so calmly that I had no idea how to respond. I was silent for a few seconds.

"Fine. Who are you, really?"

"Not so fast. I need to confirm who you are first." Her smile faded. "What were you trying to tell me at lunch?"

There was no point hiding anything now.

"I found a hidden door. Behind it there seemed to be a movie theater."

Her eyes lit up.

"Then I don't need to keep you in suspense either."

She tied her hair up into a high ponytail and gave her head a shake. All at once she looked much sharper.

"I'm an undercover agent sent by the Underground. My mission is to rescue the People undergoing treatment."

"The Underground? The organization that resists State Power?" It came out before I could think, and then I paused, as if I didn't quite understand what I had just said.

"That's right." As she spoke, she called up a floating control panel. "Have you remembered what State Power is yet?"

I shook my head.

"Then for now, all you need to know is that it's the system that runs this country."

She switched rapidly between screens.

"The most important thing right now is finding the theater. Everything else can wait."

"What is the theater, exactly?"

"An emergency liaison post. A foothold the organization has planted in the network."

She reached out and swept a finger across the panel. A thin, dark red line stretched from her fingertip and wound its way through the darkness.

I recognized it at once. That gentle curve was the shoreline of the black-sand beach; those few sharp angles were the hills I'd climbed; that violent tremor was the river I had waded through. It was the path the hummingbird had led me along.

But the line only reached as far as the cave before it broke off. The rest was just a few scattered points of light, flickering in the darkness.

C02 stared at those points, her brow furrowing deeper and deeper. She pulled the broken line forward bit by bit with her finger. It bent, shrank back, then stretched out again, as though feeling its way along an invisible road.

"Why did you take me to the cave before?"

"The cave is a liaison post too. It can help you recover your memories." She stared at the broken line and sighed. "Too bad State Power cut the process short. I went the long way around, and you still couldn't hold out for even a minute."

I ignored her complaint. "So you have my memories?"

"Yes and no." She frowned. "We have some data packages, but only you can unlock what's inside."

Before I could make sense of that, a blinking point of light suddenly appeared at the end of the line.

C02 let out a breath of relief and closed the panel.

"Let's go."

We walked toward the light. All around us was still pitch black, but as we walked, I noticed the point of light always stayed about the same distance away. I stopped, but the darkness beneath my feet didn't. It kept flowing slowly to one side, carrying me forward, as if I were standing in an invisible river.

Without looking back, C02 grabbed my wrist. "Don't stop. It won't wait for us."

Along the way, I told her everything: waking in the ICU, escaping the ward, entering the People's Therapy Center.

At that, she suddenly looked up at me. "You're sure you went to the hospital in your subconscious?"

"Of course. I was running around in there for ages."

"But I only opened the hidden door for you."

I froze. "You didn't open any other door for me?"

"No."

Then who had opened that door just before my pursuers arrived?

I looked her in the eye. "Why did you open the hidden door for me?"

She avoided my gaze and said calmly, "I used to be a nurse. Once I cared for a patient who'd had a miscarriage. She told me she wanted to give the daughter who was never born a nickname. Fawn."

Some overlooked corners of my memory suddenly pieced themselves together. That day, when my husband sat by my bed gripping my hand, the person who had been silently watching us, the one whose face I couldn't remember, was a young nurse with black hair.

We were both silent for a long time.

"Maybe it's fate," she said with a smile.

"Why do you still have your old memories?"

"Do you remember my first session?" she said.

Of course I did. That session had nearly killed her.

"My amnesia is real," she said. "It was just planned. A data package was implanted in my brain. The moment I connected to the treatment system, it unlocked automatically."

"Like how I found my name?"

She nodded. "Catching a little bird is no easy thing, you know."

I thought of how clumsily I had chased the fawn in the cave, and felt a twinge of fellow feeling.

"But with all that commotion, how come they didn't send you for a checkup?"

"By rights, I should have been sent long ago." She lowered her eyes. "The girl who died in her cell covered for me. Her mission was to poison this hospital's treatment system. Once she infected it, all my abnormal data got logged as a new patient's stress response. That's also why I've been able to open up this space inside your subconscious."

So the resemblance I'd sensed between C02 and the girl who kept asking me where the windows were hadn't been an illusion after all.

"So the Underground wiped your memory first, then hid the key inside your head?" I asked.

"Yes. But that's nothing compared to what State Power does," she said. "Every member of the People is forced into treatment at forty-five. To make you cooperate, they wipe your memory first, then hand you a reason for being treated."

"So I was never in a car accident?"

"Hard to say. Sometimes they make use of accidents that really did happen."

I thought of the displays I'd seen in that enormous room. "On the container's panel outside, the screen said I'd been injected with thirteen sets. What does that mean?"

"Simply put, complete brainwashing," she replied without looking up. "During treatment, the system injects thirty sets of special characters into your nerves. Those characters form a cocoon that filters out emotions and information deemed unnecessary."

"Unnecessary information?"

"Like the People's memories of Citizen life. Their envy of Citizens. Their dissatisfaction with reality. Any thought of resistance." She looked at me. "It makes you take your memories out of context, so you only keep the interpretations that meet requirements. In the end, you naturally come to believe the world was always right."

A chill ran down my back. "So I've already been nearly half filtered?"

"That was the plan," she said. "But when you unlocked the data package, it tore a hole in the cocoon. That's why your memories came back."

I thought of the excruciating pain in the cave that had nearly ripped my nerves apart. So that was what it felt like when the cocoon tore.

"But a hole can be patched. Once all thirty sets are done, the cocoon seals for good. After that, there's no turning back." She sighed, then went on. "But whether the treatment is forced or not, whatever the method, people all seem to want their lives to be a little easier."

I fell silent.

She was right. For the People, treatment wasn't entirely a bad thing. If forgetting could ease the pain, if you could be happy just by not thinking, then why would anyone resist?

I looked around at the pitch-black space and suddenly felt it was no different from the world outside. Maybe my husband had already been treated after failing the assessment. Maybe I had been too. Even if I really did escape the hospital, what then? Join the Underground?

I didn't even know how much of what remained in me could still be called a self.

"We're here."

C02 tapped the control panel, and a rift suddenly opened in the black space. Through it, I could dimly make out rows of dark red seats, exactly like the theater I had fallen into before.

I started to step inside, but C02 stopped me with an outstretched arm.

"Wait."

She pressed her palm against the edge of the rift. A stream of tiny symbols poured out from beneath her hand and raced along the rift's outline. She brought up the panel and compared the symbols line by line against a set of characters she had stored long ago, her lips moving silently.

"Lately, State Power has started counterfeiting liaison posts." She didn't look up. "They look exactly the same from outside, but whoever goes in never comes back out."

I held my breath and watched the symbols scroll past, line after line.

One second, two seconds...

A ring of soft green light lit up around the edge of the rift.

Only then did C02 let out a long breath. "It's real."

We went in and sat down in two random seats.

The screen lit up. In the center was a huge line of white numbers:

**06:42**

The numbers ticked down, second by second.

"This place won't last much longer," C02 said quietly.

After a burst of static, a steady male voice spoke: "My brave comrades, my fighters, welcome."

There was excitement in his voice he could barely contain: "Though I'd like to exchange a few pleasantries with you, I can't wait to share the news of our victory. In the ten-odd days you've been undercover, the world has changed beyond recognition."

C02 and I looked at each other.

"Just the day before yesterday, the Underground seized twelve of State Power's base stations. Today, we will destroy its communications satellite."

Victory had come so suddenly, yet I felt no joy at all. I turned and saw that C02's eyes were already brimming with tears.

"You must withdraw from the network at once, because all of this is about to cease to exist. In three hours, we will take over the hospital's network and set the People free!" The man could barely suppress his excitement.

C02 rose to her feet without thinking and saluted the screen.

The numbers on the screen ticked down to **02:15**.

"Any questions?"

I raised my hand.

"When did the war start?"

"The day you were admitted." His voice turned grave. "Your car accident was no accident. It was a missile strike."

In my mind, a blurred image suddenly came into focus. I saw a missile falling from the sky. The blast threw up billowing smoke and swallowed the entire car.

At last, I remembered what had happened that day.

"You were fleeing treatment at the time," the man continued. "That's why we noticed you, and decided you'd be among the first to be rescued."

I sat frozen in my seat. So that was it.

But there was one more thing I had always wanted to know. "The girl who died in our ward. How did she really die?"

The screen fell silent for a moment.

"Her cover was blown, and the system seized control of her during treatment." The man's voice dropped. "So as not to leak the plan for what comes next, she had no choice but to take her own life in her cell."

C02 bowed her head.

My throat suddenly tightened. So the girl who had died like a bird bathed in blood, poised for flight, had traded her life for all of this.

The numbers on the screen had ticked down to **00:19**.

"We're almost out of time." The man spoke faster. "I sincerely invite you to join the Underground. If you haven't made up your mind yet, you can give me your answer after we've gotten you out."

I opened my mouth, but before I could answer, the man's voice rose again, rousing: "Comrades, see you in three hours."

**00:00**

The screen, the seats, the whole theater went dark in the same instant.

# 4

I sat in my cell and said nothing for a long time.

Once I got out, how was I supposed to go on living?

I looked over at C02's cell. She had already lain back down on her bed, apparently resting up for what was to come. I looked at the others in the room, but could only see blurry shapes through the frosted glass. They were evidently still immersed in their subconscious. I didn't know whether to envy them or pity them.

I don't know how much time had passed when a dull thud sounded in the room.

I looked up sharply. Two running cells had collided.

The patients inside them didn't seem to notice at all. They went on drifting through their own subconscious.

I stared blankly at the two cells. "So they do collide after all..."

Before the words were out, there was another violent crash. This time, the whole ward shook.

C02 sat up at once and looked at me.

I looked back at her and nodded.

The rescue had begun.

By the time the cell doors began opening one after another, the battle outside had been raging for twenty minutes.

C02 showed a side of herself entirely different from usual. She immediately organized the other patients in the ward, directing everyone away from the crossfire and out through the emergency exits.

The hospital was choked with smoke, the walls riddled with bullet holes. The bodies of medical staff and Underground fighters lay scattered across the floor, and explosions sounded in the distance. I stumbled through the corridors with the crowd, so terrified by the shells and gunfire I could barely speak.

At last we boarded an Underground armored transport. As the doors closed, that familiar male voice spoke again.

"Comrades, you've been through a lot."

He shook hands with the patients one by one, telling them they were safe now, that they didn't need to be afraid anymore.

Then he walked up to C02 and shook her hand. "You took on a mission in a moment of crisis and lived up to every expectation! Thank you for everything you've done for the organization. Now our fallen comrades can rest in peace."

"Secretary..." C02 gripped his hand, tears welling in her eyes again.

Finally, he held out his hand to me. "Comrade, have you made up your mind?"

I looked at that hand. It was thick and broad, with coarse pores and dense hair, covered in calluses and scratches.

Was I really ready?

But if I didn't take it, where else could I go? Back to my husband, back to that calm and dreary life, to keep swallowing my pride and watch the things in my life taken away one by one?

My job, my child, my memories. In the end, was anything truly mine?

Maybe nothing. All of it would disappear sooner or later. Some of it was already gone.

I thought of the girl who died like a bird bathed in blood, poised for flight. I thought of the child I never got the chance to call Fawn. I thought of my husband repeating "just two points short" over and over.

I thought of every person in that hospital who was called a "patient." Like me, each of them had once had a name.

The vehicle lurched, and the armored car drove out past the hospital walls. Outside the window, the once-bustling streets lay in ruins, full of smoke, fire, and people running in every direction.

I turned to the Secretary, no longer hesitating.

I took that burning-hot hand. "I will."

The Secretary smiled and squeezed back hard.

"Good!" He turned to C02, his tone much lighter. "Oh, by the way, the line cut out so suddenly back there I didn't even get to finish. We'll have to upgrade our tech!"

C02 paused and glanced at me. "But we heard everything."

"Heard what?"

"'Comrades, see you in three hours.'"

The carriage suddenly went quiet, save for the roar of the engine.

The smile on the Secretary's face slowly froze.

"Before I got to that last line," he said slowly, "the connection had already dropped. I never said that."

C02's face went white. She opened her mouth, but not a single word came out.

The Secretary snatched up his radio. "All vehicles, change course immediately! The plan has been compromised—"

Before he could finish, a blinding white flash lit the road ahead.

The armored car in front of us was reduced to ash in an instant. Our vehicle swerved violently. I was thrown against the side of the carriage, my hand nearly slipping out of his.

But the Secretary didn't let go. Instead, he clamped down on my hand. "Comrade, the hardest hour is upon us!"

A second white flash came down.

*Boom!*

The shriek of tearing metal exploded in my ears, and the vehicle was split clean open. The ringing in my ears and the concussion left me unable to think. Freezing wind poured into the carriage, so sharp I could barely breathe.

I looked down at the hand still gripping mine. It was still burning hot, but it was no longer attached to anyone.

I screamed and flung it away.

C02 had collapsed across from me, her body pinned tight by the twisted carriage. She didn't panic or cry for help. She just looked out at the fire filling the sky, her expression calm.

She turned and looked at me, her face full of relief. "Looks like this is the ending." Her voice was very soft, and the wind swallowed it almost at once.

She closed her eyes and said nothing more.

The armored car shook violently, then the shockwave of an explosion hurled it into the air.

The world spun.

I had escaped the hospital barely five minutes ago. Was everything really going to end here?

I closed my eyes.

In an instant, I seemed to see a gray expanse, and at its far end, a glimmer like scattered diamonds.

The glimmer stretched slowly into shining star trails that wrapped around me layer upon layer, until nothing remained of the world but white.

From matter, to concept, to code, to the very edge of mathematics. In the end, even mathematics disappeared.

Only nothingness remained.

...

I woke in a daze.

Several medical staff were bustling around me, the data on the control panel changing constantly. I found myself lying inside an enormous container, my body too heavy to move.

I felt something familiar against my back. There was a bump on the inner wall of the container, pressing into me.

I had felt this before, I was sure of it. I tried hard to remember, but no matter what, I couldn't.

"You're awake?" My doctor let out a long breath of relief.

"There were a few hiccups along the way, but the treatment was a success in the end. If we hadn't saved it, I'd probably be done for, hahaha."

"You've got some nerve saying that!" another doctor cut in impatiently. "Hurry up and wheel her out. One person's been hogging this thing forever, and there are so many people waiting in line!"

My doctor gave a sheepish laugh. "Come on, I earned some credit this time, didn't I? If I hadn't opened the door for her right when I did and let her walk herself back, the line never would have connected so smoothly. This time, not a single one got away."

"You're the one who lost her in the first place. Hurry up!"

The container slowly opened.

I sat in a hovering wheelchair and was pushed into a sunlit corridor. Warm light fell over me as my doctor walked beside me.

I looked at everything around me and suddenly felt it was all completely unfamiliar. "Where am I?"

"You're back in the real world now," he explained with a smile. "Think about it. You should still remember what happened in the virtual world just now, but while you were there, you couldn't remember anything from this side."

I nodded.

But those memories were already in pieces.

All I could recall were scattered images: a green forest, a black-sand beach, a blue cave, a dark red theater, and a girl's face...

"After all, you lived through a month in just a few minutes," the doctor said. "Remembering this much is pretty good! Some people can't even remember anything except that they went in."

I froze. "Only a few minutes have passed?"

"Well, not quite that short," he corrected. "It's been half an hour since you entered the therapy center."

He pushed open a door. "Don't think so much. Your husband is waiting for you."

In the warm, amber-lit room behind the door stood a man. It was my husband. He was much thinner than I remembered, but he still wore that gentle smile. He bent down and held me softly.

"Honey, how do you feel after the treatment?"

"Not bad." I leaned against him. "It doesn't hurt."

In fact, looking at his face, I had never felt so happy.

"I've got a meeting, so I'll leave you two alone." My doctor backed out of the room, beaming, and quietly closed the door.

My husband and I sat in the sunlight, nestled together in silence.

Everything in the past seemed to have drifted away. The war, the conspiracies, the memories, and the truth I had once longed so desperately to know, all of it now felt distant and hazy.

Suddenly I felt that maybe I really didn't need to remember anything anymore.

Outside the window, a small dark red bird flew past.

I turned my head and watched it pass through the treetops, rise through layer after layer of clouds, and fly to where my gaze could not follow.

Above the clouds, there seemed to be a vast black shadow. It hung there in silence, as if it covered the entire sky.

I stared at it for a while.

For some reason, I had lost all interest in finding out what they were.

So I looked away and turned to my husband.

"So," I asked with a smile, "how shall we enjoy life from here?"
