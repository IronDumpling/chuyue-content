# This Is Us, Under Today's Atomized Society

Went to see the rerelease of this one at Imagine Cinema on Carlton recently, with a few friends who love film as much as I do. Great experience — this rewatch was the first time it really hit me how black-comedy this film actually is. We were laughing pretty much the whole way through.

![The showtime screen for American Psycho at Imagine Cinema](/images/blog/films/american-psycho-review-1.jpg)

Watching it again in the theater, I realized how closely its takes on appearance, consumerism, materialism, sex, and death mirror a lot of my own fantasies. It's also remarkably close to the core of a short film script I wrote, Night Run. I've always loved other films from the same era that dissect the manic inner lives of urban white-collar professionals — Fight Club, American Beauty, and the like. Fight Club used to be the best film in the world, as far as I was concerned.

I want to dig into two directions here: first, a kind of psychoanalysis of myself, and by extension, of the single young urbanites I think I represent; second, a critique and revision of Night Run. I'll start with the self-analysis. There's a scene in American Psycho I related to hard — Bateman is on the phone, going about business, while porn plays in the background as ambient noise for whatever he's rushing to get through. I've genuinely done that, more than once, and I've actually tried to work out why. There's also this: East Asian society increasingly demands that men be polished and put-together, but men and women read that demand completely differently. Some men take it as a sigma-male thing — I only answer to myself. Others take it as a means of acquiring sexual capital. Women, meanwhile, tend to read it as male peacocking. My summary here is probably not fully accurate.

I want to reflect on this now — in a lot of ways, I'm a lot like Bateman. I work out. I'm learning skincare, makeup, and how to dress. I take pride in my work. Like him, I'll go on and on about music and records to show I have taste — I'll even recommend records to any friend who comes over, and brag about all the concerts I've been to. I curate my social media carefully, polishing my own "display surface." I feel like a lot of East Asian culture works this way — anything meant to project that you have substance can get commodified by consumerism into a symbol.

I used to be a fairly introverted person; now I lean a lot more extroverted. I feel like more things should be put on display, because we live in an image-driven, broadcast-era world — you have to carefully package yourself before others can get to know you. Still, I've kept a lot of introverted habits — I still like to keep some of what I want to express slightly hidden, so only the people who really want to find it, will. These days I also buy furniture to better display things around my apartment — vinyl records, figures, books, and so on. My weekend activities have gradually expanded too; I used to prefer watching videos alone, watching films, working on projects by myself. I feel like I'm walking a pretty sunlit path, but I also worry whether I've fully bought into that sweet, barren trap that capitalism, consumerism, and neoliberalism want me to fall into.

I agree that the protagonist's copycat-killer motive in Night Run feels a bit disconnected. Let me talk about myself instead — maybe that'll surface some more interesting motives.

I've always been someone who likes secretly doing bad things — not necessarily anything truly unconscionable, because I know not to do those. What I like most is doing gray-area things, crossing lines where no one's watching — like sneaking off to meet girls I met on dating apps or social platforms behind my parents' backs.

After coming to Canada, I realized some of my time was entirely my own — no one looking for me, no one restricting me — so I'd want to do something secret, something that was just mine. It made me feel free, comfortable. It's the feeling of an aimless walk on a cool, overcast day, a light rain soaking into every inch of your skin, breathing in the fresh air, singing at the top of your lungs into an empty sky.

That's why I wrote the break-in scene for the protagonist.

As for the wire fraud — that's my own real experience. I really was scammed, and it was through a scheme that had me play the role of an "investigation assistant" in a financial crime. I fully believed it was real at the time. Partly because, as a student cut off from society, that whole experience is what got me into true crime stories afterward — I wanted to understand how dark the world could really be, and stay alert to worse things happening.

This also ties into the huge stretch of empty land near where I used to live in Markham. I always felt like you could die there and no one would ever know — the same way no one knows I live here (even though I actually have a lot of friends). Back in high school, I used to fantasize that if I died during evening study hall, no one would notice even after it ended, which struck me as darkly ironic (even though, honestly, I have a pretty high sense of presence). But I was afraid that maybe I just thought too highly of myself, that I actually don't matter, that nobody really needs me.

And because of that, I feel like I can do a lot of things I thought I couldn't — chasing a kind of thrill.
