_The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping_ is a fairly average film overall, but it has some genuinely great moments, especially in its second half.

# **The Arena**

The arena’s production design is gorgeous. With its vibrant colors and blooming flowers, it almost feels like something out of _Midsommar_. The volcanic eruption is visually spectacular, too. Unfortunately, the action itself is rather underwhelming. So many tributes are killed off by environmental hazards that we barely get any memorable fights, rivalries, or strategic confrontations. The Games end up feeling more like PvE than PvP.

That said, the opening of the Games is incredibly tense, with a nice callback to the original film’s warning about stepping off the platform too early. Most of the other scenes that stuck with me actually come after the Games, particularly Haymitch’s victory ceremony and his journey home.

# **Rebellion as Spectacle**

One of the things I’ve always found fascinating about _The Hunger Games_ is how the series explores reality television, political propaganda, and the manipulation of public perception.

Haymitch sets out to rebel against the Games, even if it means sacrificing his own life. But the Capitol doesn’t just control whether he lives or dies. It also controls how the audience interprets his rebellion.

The producers turn him into a rebellious bad-boy celebrity. He’s dangerous, defiant, and willing to challenge authority, but he’s also cool, charismatic, and endlessly entertaining. His rebellion becomes a spectacle, something audiences can enjoy without ever feeling threatened by it.

This reminds me of punk culture, especially cyberpunk. What begins as a rejection of the establishment can eventually be absorbed by the very commercial system it once opposed, repackaged as an aesthetic to be bought and sold. People get to admire the image of rebellion without ever confronting the system being rebelled against.

The victory ceremony near the end is particularly effective. We’ve witnessed everything Haymitch actually went through, only to see the Capitol edit and repackage those events into a triumphant television narrative.

In the Capitol’s version, Haymitch is no longer someone who tried to sacrifice himself and protect his allies. Instead, he’s a cunning, self-serving troublemaker who betrayed his teammates, manipulated everyone around him, and defeated his enemies to claim victory. Every genuine act of sacrifice or resistance is reinterpreted as another entertaining moment in his carefully manufactured persona.

Even more disturbing is what happens to Louella McCoy, the young girl from District 12 who dies in an accident on the way to the Games. Snow simply replaces her with another girl, Lou Lou, who has been conditioned to impersonate her. As far as the public is concerned, Louella never died. She simply carries on playing her part.

One girl’s death is erased, another girl’s identity is taken away, and the audience watching at home has absolutely no idea.

I do wish the film had leaned further into the horror of this reveal. The replacement girl’s appearance could have been genuinely unsettling, almost like something out of a psychological horror film. It would have made the idea of reality being completely rewritten even more disturbing.

Even at the very end, when Haymitch detonates a bomb after winning, hoping to complete his rebellion through his own death, he is denied control over his fate. The Capitol saves him because it needs him alive, a victorious celebrity who can be endlessly exploited.

But it doesn’t let him live in peace, either. The people he loves are killed one after another, until he becomes too afraid to let anyone get close to him. Eventually, he turns into the bitter, isolated drunk that everyone in District 12 avoids.

**Haymitch is denied not only the right to rebel, but even ownership of his own suffering. His pain becomes another trophy for the people who rule over him.**

# **The Legacy of Those Who Failed**

This brings me to the final homecoming sequence, which is easily my favorite part of the film.

Haymitch goes from a young man determined to change the system to the cynical, alcoholic, emotionally distant mentor we know from the original trilogy.

There’s a particularly cruel irony in the fact that his birthday falls on Reaping Day. As he puts it, his birthday present every year is two more tributes from District 12, sent off to the arena.

He can never truly escape the Games. Even after winning, he’s forced to relive his trauma year after year, for the rest of his life.

One of my favorite supporting characters is the young Plutarch Heavensbee, played by Jesse Plemons. Working as a cameraman within the Capitol’s propaganda machine, he nevertheless becomes an important influence on Haymitch’s growing desire to rebel. He eventually goes on to become the Head Gamemaker in the original trilogy, as well as a key figure in the rebellion.

There’s also a wonderful piece of casting trivia here. Plutarch was originally played by Philip Seymour Hoffman, who actually played Jesse Plemons’ father in Paul Thomas Anderson’s _The Master_. Now, the two actors portray the same character at different points in his life. There’s something genuinely moving about that kind of continuity, both within the story and across cinema itself.

Plutarch helps Haymitch understand that even if their generation cannot succeed, they can still do something for those who come after them. So Haymitch never completely gives up. As a mentor, he keeps searching, year after year, for someone who might finally have the courage and the opportunity to fight back.

And eventually, after all those years, Katniss arrives.

We finally get to see Jennifer Lawrence’s Katniss and Woody Harrelson’s Haymitch together on the big screen again, after so many years. It’s a surprisingly emotional moment. Thinking back to when the first _Hunger Games_ film came out, none of us could have imagined just how much history would eventually be built around these characters.

I’ve always had a soft spot for stories about ideals and struggles being passed down across generations. _Orb: On the Movements of the Earth_, _Arknights: Babel_, and _Rogue One: A Star Wars Story_ are some of my favorite examples.

To me, the most moving thing about a prequel isn’t how much lore it adds. It’s how it reveals what the people who came before had to sacrifice to make the story we already know possible. Sometimes a character only needs a handful of memorable scenes, or even just a familiar name or title, for their appearance in a prequel to complete an emotional arc that spans the entire series.

More importantly, the people in these stories often have no idea whether their efforts will ever amount to anything.

**But we, the audience, already know.**

There’s something deeply moving to me about this kind of idealism, where one generation carries on the unfinished work of another. Those who came before may fail, sacrifice everything, or spend their entire lives without ever seeing the world they fought for. But that doesn’t make their efforts meaningless. The next generation inherits not only what they accomplished, but also the dreams they never got to fulfill.

It reminds me of the ideals of solidarity and continuity that often run through stories of revolutionary history. What one person cannot accomplish in their lifetime can become the work of those who follow. People die, but their ideals can endure across generations.

Haymitch never became the person who changed the world.

But he lived long enough to meet someone who could.

**And in the end, a lifetime of failure became the foundation for someone else’s victory.**
