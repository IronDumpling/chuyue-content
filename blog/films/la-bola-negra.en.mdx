_A black ball, a snowfall;_
_A firework, a play;_
_A bullet, a poem;_
_A farewell, a letter._

I went into _The Black Ball_ with very high expectations. Several friends who had seen it at TIFF had strongly recommended it, calling it one of the best films of the year.

I came away with mixed feelings. There is a great deal to admire here. The cinematography, mise-en-scène, and sheer expressive power of several key sequences are exceptional. Yet I also found the storytelling somewhat disappointing, as though the film never quite realized the full potential of its own material.

# **The Visual Splendor of Classical Cinema**

The opening sequence immediately establishes the extraordinary level of craftsmanship on display.

An ensemble of villagers in a Spanish mountain village. A band performing on a hillside. A mother who has just arrived. The distant sound of an approaching aircraft. Then, without warning, bombs begin to fall, and death descends upon the village.

What I particularly love about this sequence is its use of a static camera and deep staging. The musicians occupy the foreground, while the aircraft initially appears as little more than a tiny speck in the distance. As the roar of its engines grows louder, the plane gradually becomes more prominent, until it begins dropping bombs and strafing the crowd. Within moments, the lively village is thrown into chaos.

It reminded me of the famous deep-focus compositions in _Citizen Kane_. Rather than constantly moving the camera or cutting between different shot scales, the scene makes full use of foreground, middle ground, and background, allowing characters and events in different parts of the frame to unfold simultaneously. Every element has clearly been choreographed with tremendous precision, yet the result feels remarkably natural. There is something almost theatrical about the ingenuity of this mise-en-scène.

Another unforgettable image follows: Sebastián climbing through the ruins toward a statue of Saint Sebastian, its body pierced by arrows, and leaning toward it as though about to kiss it. Soon afterward, he flees, is discovered by Nationalist soldiers, and is forced to join their ranks to survive.

The opening air raid reminded me of Emir Kusturica’s _Underground_. In terms of sheer intricacy and staging, I might even prefer the opening of _The Black Ball_.

The cinematography remains outstanding throughout. The camera movements are assured, the compositions meticulous, and the colors warm and richly textured. It looks, in many ways, like the classical cinema I remember.

It has become increasingly rare to see a film with this kind of Golden Age epic sensibility. Rather than relying on rapid editing or exaggerated camera movement to generate emotion, it is willing to hold a composition, sustain a long take, and arrange action across multiple planes of depth, allowing actors to perform within a coherent physical space.

Yet the film is by no means entirely classical in its approach. Moving between its three timelines, it employs numerous match cuts, sound bridges, match-on-action transitions, and inventive transitions built around light and shadow. These techniques not only allow the stories to flow seamlessly across decades, but also create subtle echoes between people and events separated by time.

One particularly beautiful example comes when Sebastián, overwhelmed by grief, plays his trumpet alone in the bathroom. The sound carries across the cut to Alberto returning to his hometown. It feels almost as though someone from the past were calling out to a descendant, summoning him home to confront a history he has yet to understand.

# **Three Timelines and a Long Road to the Story**

The film unfolds across three timelines: 1932, 1937, and 2017.

The 1932 storyline is brightly colored, meticulously composed, and deliberately theatrical. The wartime narrative of 1937 adopts a more naturalistic style, while the 2017 sequences are cooler in tone, with a greater sense of emotional distance between the characters.

Each storyline has a distinctive and compelling introduction. Initially, I found the interweaving of the three narratives exhilarating. But as the film progressed, it gradually settled into an extended period of exposition.

There are still several remarkable sequences: a long take of Rafael crawling out from a pile of corpses, a succession of close-ups as surviving prisoners are asked to identify themselves, and the extraordinary scene of Carlos singing and dancing in a tavern.

The tavern sequence, in particular, is practically a complete theatrical performance. The choreography, the coordination of the extras, the music, and the camera movements are all intricately arranged. At first, I found it wonderfully inventive and entertaining.

Of the three narratives, I was most drawn to the events of 1937. Yet the clearest narrative hook belongs to 2017, which is tasked from the beginning with uncovering the secrets of the past.

In 2017, Alberto is away on a business trip when he meets a man through a dating app and spends the night with him. Shortly afterward, he receives a phone call informing him that his grandfather has died and left him an inheritance.

When he later discusses his grandfather’s death with his mother, the conversation unexpectedly provokes a strong reaction. They argue and part on bad terms. Only later, encouraged by his male partner, does Alberto finally travel to the countryside to meet his grandfather’s lawyer and claim the inheritance.

It is easy to sense that some secret lies behind the inheritance, and that Alberto’s grandfather is probably connected to one of the earlier timelines. Revealing his identity seems likely to bring the apparently separate narratives together.

Unfortunately, although the 2017 storyline establishes a clear mystery, it takes an awfully long time to get moving. The film devotes considerable attention to Alberto’s everyday life, his strained relationship with his mother, and his eventual decision to travel to the countryside, without allowing the investigation itself to make much progress.

Meanwhile, the 1932 and 1937 narratives wander through the everyday lives of numerous characters, incorporating side stories and historical references that I often struggled to connect with emotionally.

As a result, watching the film gradually became an exercise in appreciating its technique. I could still admire the framing, staging, and editing of individual scenes, but I found it increasingly difficult to invest in the characters’ fates.

It is only when Alberto discovers a photograph in his grandfather’s house and realizes that his grandfather was Sebastián from the 1937 storyline that the film finally delivers on its earlier mystery.

And with that revelation, the long, unknown remainder of Sebastián’s life becomes a mystery of its own.

# **A Lost Manuscript**

The film then arrives at its second major revelation.

Alberto opens the envelope containing his grandfather’s inheritance and discovers a manuscript of _The Black Ball_.

Until this moment, the 1932 storyline has been presented as though it were another historical reality unfolding alongside the other two narratives. Suddenly, we realize that what we have been watching all along is a play.

The deliberately theatrical performances, the almost unreal brightness of the colors, the exaggerated physical gestures, and Carlos’s elaborate song-and-dance sequence in the tavern all acquire a new explanation.

The two revelations connect 1937 to 2017, and then 1932 to the other timelines. What initially appeared to be three parallel narratives finally converges, transforming the film into a structure in which theatrical fiction and historical reality are nested within one another.

New questions immediately arise. Who wrote the play? Why did Sebastián possess it? Why did he leave it to his grandson? And what does it have to do with the events of 1937?

As Alberto’s investigation continues, we gradually learn that the manuscript is connected to _The Black Ball_, an unfinished work by the Spanish poet Federico García Lorca.

It was only after the screening, while researching the film, that I learned Lorca and his partner, Rafael Rodríguez Rapún, were both real historical figures.

Lorca was murdered by Nationalist forces following the outbreak of the Spanish Civil War in 1936, while Rafael died from wartime injuries in 1937. The romantic relationship between Sebastián and Rafael, as well as Rafael’s execution, are fictional elements woven into this historical setting.

Even more intriguingly, Lorca really did leave behind an unfinished work titled _The Black Ball_, but only four pages survive. The complete script shown in the film, along with the discovery of its supposedly lost manuscript, is an invention of the filmmakers.

Still, I found myself somewhat dissatisfied with the play-within-the-film.

The 1932 narrative follows Carlos, a young gay man whose sexuality has become an open secret in his town. Because of this, he is denied admission to the local Casino club, an institution dominated by the town’s traditional elites, including religious figures, wealthy businessmen, and military officers.

The club’s members vote with black and white balls to determine whether an applicant deserves entry into their exclusive circle.

It is a fairly straightforward political allegory. The black ball represents rejection, the club represents the ruling class and its power to determine social belonging, and Carlos represents an individual excluded because of his identity.

The play reaches its climax when Carlos denies being gay during his trial. The building where he is being judged is subsequently destroyed in an uprising, and Carlos enters a surreal final sequence.

As a play-within-a-film, however, I had hoped it would establish a more intricate relationship with the events of 1937.

Perhaps the characters, choices, and betrayals within the play could have found more specific counterparts in Sebastián and Rafael’s lives. Or perhaps some seemingly insignificant detail in the theatrical narrative could have acquired an entirely new meaning through the historical storyline.

Instead, the relationship between the two remains largely thematic.

In the play, the oppressors are overthrown and Carlos achieves a kind of liberation. In reality, Rafael is executed, while Sebastián must continue living under a dictatorship.

Perhaps this contrast is precisely the point: theater can offer people the endings that reality denies them.

Even so, I felt the parallels remained somewhat broad. The revelation of the play’s fictional nature is structurally ingenious, but its relationship to the lives of the characters in the historical narrative never became quite as rich as I had hoped.

# **The Lives Left Outside the Frame**

What I regret even more is the film’s decision not to explore Sebastián’s life between 1937 and 2017 in greater depth.

The central narrative of 1937 is actually quite simple.

Sebastián is forced into the Nationalist army during the war and assigned to guard Rafael, a Republican prisoner. He gradually develops feelings for Rafael and attempts to protect him, but ultimately finds himself compelled to carry out the order to execute him.

After learning that Rafael is about to be shot, Sebastián rushes back from the seaside to his bedside.

Rafael lies facing away from him, his bare back still marked by traces of blood. Sebastián reaches out, wanting to touch his skin, but cannot bring himself to make contact.

His hand hovers above Rafael’s back for what feels like an eternity, then slowly moves toward his ear, the nape of his neck, and his shoulder. Again and again, he reaches forward, coming within the smallest distance of touching him, only to stop.

Finally, his hand falls to the edge of the bed, and Sebastián covers his face and weeps. Rafael awakens and reaches out to comfort him instead.

There is no heated argument, no explicit declaration of love, not even a line of dialogue. Yet the emotional intimacy and the impossible distance between these two men are conveyed with extraordinary precision.

They are physically close, yet separated by two worlds that cannot be reconciled.

One is a Nationalist soldier, the other a Republican prisoner. One belongs to the army of the persecutors, while the other is an enemy awaiting execution. Sebastián loves Rafael, but he cannot escape his own position, much less prevent the death that is approaching.

His hand reaches forward and withdraws, again and again, as though political allegiance, military discipline, and fear have become an invisible wall between their bodies.

What makes the scene especially moving is that Rafael is ultimately the one who completes the gesture.

The man who is about to die comforts the man who must go on living.

One of Sebastián’s fellow soldiers witnesses this moment. Later, when Sebastián secretly visits Rafael before the execution, it is this same soldier who chooses not to interfere, allowing them a final moment alone.

Rafael gives Sebastián his last requests. He asks him to deliver a letter to his family, to tell his younger siblings to study hard and live well, and to find the manuscript of _The Black Ball_.

In the end, Sebastián personally executes Rafael.

After the war, he manages to recover the manuscript, although four pages are accidentally left behind.

But Sebastián’s life is far from over.

He must live through decades of Franco’s dictatorship. To survive within the social expectations of the time, he marries a woman, has a daughter, and conceals his sexuality. Later, he meets someone he truly loves and eventually abandons his wife and daughter to begin a new life with his male partner, living quietly in the countryside.

None of this is depicted directly. Instead, these decades are pieced together through Alberto’s investigation in 2017 and the fragmented recollections of Sebastián’s descendants.

In some ways, this unexplored life may be even more complicated than the wartime story the film chooses to show us.

Sebastián is both an agent and a victim of political violence. He kills the man he loves with his own hands, then preserves the manuscript left behind by that man’s lover. He enters a heterosexual marriage in order to survive, and later finally gains the freedom to pursue love, only to inflict another kind of suffering on the wife and daughter he leaves behind.

This complexity goes far beyond the image of a straightforward victim of persecution. His life alone could have been the subject of an entire film.

Of course, there is value in leaving things unsaid.

For Alberto in 2017, the lives of previous generations are inevitably composed of secrets, photographs, inherited objects, and incomplete memories.

Yet the film spans nearly a century of history while leaving the most complicated decades of Sebastián’s life outside the frame. We can imagine what he went through from the scattered clues, but we rarely get to witness how those experiences transformed him.

That, to me, is a small but significant missed opportunity.

# **A World of Snow**

Of all the images in _The Black Ball_, the one that has stayed with me most is the snowfall at the end of the play.

Earlier, while Carlos is dancing flamenco in a tavern, he encounters a disoriented elderly woman who mistakes him for her dead son, a boy who was drowned after being pushed into the water because of his effeminate mannerisms.

She tells Carlos how pale her child had been, like a boy made of snow, so delicate that he seemed liable to melt if anyone came too close.

Later, before Carlos agrees to face the club’s judgment again and prepares to deny his homosexuality in order to gain acceptance, one of his gay friends says something remarkably similar: sometimes Carlos seems like snow, as though merely approaching him or speaking to him might cause him to melt.

At first, these exchanges seem like little more than poetic conversations between characters. But in the play’s conclusion, they acquire an entirely new significance.

The building where Carlos is being judged is destroyed in an uprising, and Carlos is knocked unconscious.

When he awakens, the world has been covered in snow.

The once-powerful elites, the club that judged him, and the entire social order surrounding his life have all disappeared beneath this sudden snowfall.

Carlos emerges from the ruins and walks alone toward the distant mountains.

Snow is such a strange and wonderful thing.

In my own novel, _As I Lay Dying_, I use snowfall as a sign of the apocalypse.

Growing up in Chongqing, snow always felt like an anomaly to me, something that came from outside the world, something that did not quite belong to it.

Snow commands everyone’s attention. Ordinary life seems to stop moving forward. Nothing else matters anymore, because the entire world’s gaze is drawn toward those strange white fragments drifting slowly down from the sky.

In _The Black Ball_, snow becomes a metaphor that follows Carlos throughout his story.

The drowned snow child and Carlos are alike: both are rejected by the world around them because they are different.

To the grieving mother, her snow child was beautiful and innocent, but also unbearably fragile, as though he might disappear at any moment. She wanted to protect him, to hold him close, yet even approaching him seemed capable of making him melt. In the end, she could only watch helplessly as he was taken from her.

When Carlos’s friend compares him to snow, the metaphor seems to acquire another meaning.

Carlos’s fragility comes not only from the persecution he faces, but also from his fear of his own identity. He longs to be understood and accepted, yet is terrified of revealing himself. If someone gets too close, the life he has so carefully constructed might melt away like snow.

And so, when he finally stands before his judges and denies being gay, it is almost as though the snow child has already begun to disappear.

But the film refuses to end his story there.

Carlos climbs alone toward the summit through the endless snow. Suddenly, countless skiers rush past him like ghosts.

Where have they come from? Where are they going?

In this world that has broken free from reality, everything becomes mysterious and impossible to explain.

Eventually, overcome by the cold, Carlos collapses in the snow and hears a voice calling to him, almost like the voice of Mother Earth herself.

Intriguingly, the voice belongs to the same actress who played the grieving mother in the tavern.

The woman who once mistook Carlos for her dead son now seems to have become the snowfield itself, finally reunited with her lost child.

And Carlos, too, has finally become a true snow child.

He no longer needs to fear melting, because he has become one with the snow.

Here, snow represents both death and acceptance.

Carlos could never find a place for himself in the society he inhabited. But in this surreal ending, he is finally embraced by a world far greater than the one that rejected him.

The tragedy is that this acceptance comes too late, and perhaps only through death.

I also love the film’s use of color in this sequence.

Throughout the story, a black ball signifies rejection, while a white ball signifies acceptance.

Carlos is rejected by the black ball held in the hands of the powerful, only to be embraced by an overwhelming expanse of white snow.

And the people and institutions that once claimed the authority to determine his fate are buried beneath that same snowfall.

Those who believed they could decide who deserved a place in society are ultimately reduced to something insignificant beneath the snow.

The origin of this snowfall may also be traced to a black-and-white flashback involving Rafael and Lorca.

Rafael once tells Lorca that he is incapable of writing anything without poetry, and that he will probably end up making it snow even in summer.

And so, a casual remark from the real world eventually finds its way into Carlos’s story.

Theater fulfills a wish that reality could not grant.

And the poem Lorca wrote for Rafael becomes, at last, a lullaby for Carlos.

# **The Queer Heart Is an Ocean Full of Secrets**

Ultimately, _The Black Ball_ is a film about queer identity, memory, and the passing of struggles from one generation to the next.

The perseverance, repression, suffering, and deaths endured by generations living through war, dictatorship, and social persecution helped make possible the greater freedoms enjoyed by sexual minorities in 2017.

But freedom does not mean that the wounds of the past simply disappear.

Some forms of trauma are passed down through generations, while others emerge from the circumstances of a new era.

Sebastián eventually gains the freedom to live with the man he loves, but his decision to abandon his wife and daughter leaves an irreparable wound in the next generation.

That wound is then passed down through Alberto’s mother.

In trying to escape the suffering imposed on him by his own time, Sebastián ultimately inflicts another kind of suffering on his descendants.

This intergenerational conflict prevents the film from becoming merely a story about resisting oppression and achieving freedom.

It also reminds us that people who have won their own freedom can still hurt others through the choices they make.

There is another seemingly incidental subplot that I found interesting.

While serving in the army, Sebastián watches a female singer perform and later speaks with her. She reminisces about the cabaret scene in prewar Madrid and mentions a drag performer who had once been enormously popular.

During the screening, I could not quite understand how this conversation related to the main story.

Only afterward did I discover that the performer she mentioned, Edmond de Bries, was a real Spanish female impersonator who enjoyed considerable popularity in the early twentieth century.

Perhaps the filmmakers are using this detail to remind us that Spain had a vibrant culture of gender expression and queer performance before the war and dictatorship.

History does not necessarily move in a straight line toward greater freedom. Things that people once possessed can also be taken away by political upheaval.

These historical references, which sometimes seem disconnected from the main narrative, therefore have significance of their own.

But for viewers unfamiliar with the historical context, they may not have their intended impact during the screening itself.

I am not a member of the LGBTQ+ community, and my knowledge of Spain’s queer history is limited. Many of these details only became clear to me after researching the film.

That undoubtedly shaped my viewing experience.

Still, I do not believe a film’s emotional power should depend entirely on how much historical knowledge its audience brings into the theater.

**Final Thoughts**

**7/10**

The cinematography, mise-en-scène, and editing in _The Black Ball_ are exceptional, and several sequences are genuinely breathtaking. The film also captures a sense of epic grandeur that I rarely encounter in contemporary cinema, recalling the visual ambition of classical historical epics.

Narratively, however, its extended buildup weakened my engagement, and the relationship between the play-within-the-film and the historical narrative never became as intricate as I had hoped.

Most of all, the vast stretches of Sebastián and Rafael’s lives left unexplored made me feel that the film had even more stories worth telling.

I was deeply impressed by its visual achievements, but never entirely convinced by its storytelling.

And yet, I suspect certain images will stay with me for a very long time.

The aircraft slowly approaching above the mountain village.

The arrow-pierced Saint Sebastian standing amid the ruins.

Carlos dancing in the tavern.

A hand hovering above a lover’s back, never quite daring to touch.

A solitary figure wandering through the snow-covered mountains.

And a poem written for someone who is gone.

After all, cinema’s power should never come from storytelling alone, should it?
