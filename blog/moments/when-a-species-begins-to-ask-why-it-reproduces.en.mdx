# **When a Species Begins to Ask Why It Reproduces**

Recently, I keep coming back to one question: **Why is it that the more developed humanity becomes, the less willing people seem to be to have children?**

If we look at this only through our own everyday experience, it is easy to explain it away by saying that “young people are under too much pressure,” “housing is too expensive,” or “raising children costs too much.” But if we widen our view to the entire world, we can see that this is actually part of a demographic transition that is happening right now.

According to the United Nations _World Population Prospects 2024_, the global total fertility rate has fallen from 3.31 births per woman in 1990 to around 2.25 in 2024. At the same time, although the world’s population is still growing, the UN projects that it will peak at around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s before beginning to decline. In other words, humanity may be going through a very long process: first a population explosion, then gradually slowing population growth, and eventually population decline.

Different countries are currently at very different points along this curve.

In 2024, Nigeria’s total fertility rate was around 4.4, meaning that a woman still has more than four children on average; India had already fallen to around 2.0, close to the replacement level; the United States was around 1.6; Canada around 1.3; and China had fallen to around 1.0. Put these numbers together and something quite interesting emerges: they do not simply correspond to “poor” and “rich.” Rather, they seem to reflect different societies being at different stages of the demographic transition.

More interestingly, population size does not immediately stop growing when fertility declines. India is already close to replacement-level fertility, but because of the enormous young population accumulated over the past several decades, its population will continue to grow for some time. Nigeria is in a completely different position. The United Nations projects that its population will continue to grow rapidly from around 233 million in 2024 to around 376 million by 2054. Meanwhile, China’s population has already begun to decline. Under the UN’s medium projection, China’s population could fall to around 1.26 billion by 2050 and then further to around 630 million by the end of the century.

And so a question that would have been almost unimaginable in the past has begun to emerge.

For most of human history, the problem we faced was **how to keep more children alive**. Disease, famine, war, and infant mortality constantly wiped out a large proportion of those who were born. Modern medicine, vaccines, clean water, agricultural technology, and public health have solved a large part of this problem. Mortality fell, and populations began to grow rapidly.

But now, some societies are beginning to face the exact opposite problem:

**If humanity has become capable of keeping the vast majority of children alive, why are more and more people choosing not to have them?**

This makes me wonder whether population decline is not merely an economic problem. Perhaps it is something that naturally emerges once a civilization reaches a certain stage of development.

## **The Tension Between Intelligence and Reproduction**

I am not saying that “smart people do not have children.” Reality is obviously much more complicated than that. Declining fertility is connected to urbanization, education, women’s employment, contraception, housing costs, the cost of raising children, social security, and changes in family structure, among many other factors.

But when all these factors are put together, an interesting transformation still becomes visible:

**The more intelligence humans possess, the more capable we become of turning reproduction from an instinctive behavior into a life decision that requires conscious thought.**

Animals do not hold family meetings to discuss whether their current economic circumstances are suitable for raising the next generation. They do not calculate how much time raising a child will require, nor do they consider whether having children might affect their career prospects. They certainly display complex forms of parental care, but they do not turn the question of “Should I reproduce?” itself into something requiring philosophical justification.

Humans do.

Natural selection originally needed only a very simple program:

**Survive, then reproduce.**

But the human brain is complicated enough to add another layer to that program:

**Why?**

And once a living creature begins asking “Why?”, it is no longer completely governed by its original biological programming.

This question was portrayed in a particularly crude, and even somewhat vicious, way in the film _Idiocracy_. The film imagines an absurd future in which intelligent, educated people become increasingly reluctant to reproduce because they are too cautious, while people who give little thought to the consequences continue having children. Several hundred years later, human society has gradually come to be dominated by “idiots.”

This is obviously not a serious demographic theory. Intelligence, education, and fertility cannot simply be reduced to a single causal relationship in the real world. But the thought experiment is interesting precisely because it magnifies a subtle question:

**If a species possesses enough intelligence to consciously decide whether or not to reproduce, then intelligence itself may become a constraint on the instinct to reproduce.**

_Attack on Titan_ presents this question in an even more extreme way. In the series, Zeke Yeager proposes the “Euthanasia Plan,” preventing Eldians from reproducing so that they would gradually disappear naturally, thereby resolving all the conflicts and disputes surrounding them.

What I find truly disturbing about it is not simply the idea that “everyone will stop having children.” It is the deeper question of whether, if a highly self-aware species can consciously decide whether it should continue reproducing, reproduction is still something that must be accomplished at all.

If someone believes that much of humanity’s suffering comes from the fact that humans keep being born, then “not being born” might seem like a complete solution.

But then comes the question: **Does a person who has not yet been born have the right to have the previous generation decide, “You should not exist”?**

At that point, this is no longer a demographic question. It becomes a question about freedom, life, and existence itself.

Modern society has even begun to separate several things that were once tightly bound together: sex does not necessarily mean love; love does not necessarily mean marriage; marriage does not necessarily mean reproduction; and reproduction is no longer a task that every person is expected to complete in life.

Humanity has genuinely acquired the ability to separate these things from one another on a massive scale. Contraception, modern medicine, social security, women’s education, and economic independence have made it possible for people to live without taking on the traditional structure of family life.

That is an extraordinary achievement. But from another perspective, it also means that **humans are becoming increasingly capable of going against their original biological logic.**

## **From Animal Society to Modern Society**

And so I started thinking: perhaps thinking itself is the original sin. If we simply did not think, maybe none of these problems would exist.

But what would happen if we really put humanity back into the animal world?

I imagine things would become very simple, and very cruel.

There is no committee in animal societies responsible for distributing reproductive opportunities equally. In some species, a small number of males obtain a large share of mating opportunities, while many other males have almost no chance to reproduce at all. Reproductive resources can be highly concentrated, and competition can be extremely intense.

They do not discuss whether this is fair, because it is simply competition.

This makes me think of a very uncomfortable but interesting question: if modern civilization really disappeared and humans returned to a society in which resources and reproductive opportunities were determined primarily by physical condition, strength, appearance, and social status, many modern people might actually find themselves at a significant disadvantage.

If I think about myself, I suspect I would be at quite a disadvantage in such a society. I am not particularly tall, my physical condition is fairly ordinary, I am nearsighted, I am not especially good-looking, and I have a few congenital imperfections. If mate selection depended primarily on physical characteristics, all of these things could be magnified enormously.

The truly remarkable thing about modern civilization is precisely that it has expanded the dimensions of human competition beyond the physical body and into culture.

Someone with fairly ordinary physical characteristics can gain social status through knowledge, writing, programming, photography, music, creativity, professional ability, a sense of humor, or any number of other abilities.

**You do not necessarily have to be an exceptional animal to become a valuable human being.**

In this sense, civilization has given humanity a large number of “secondary traits” that did not originally exist.

But civilization has not eliminated competition for mates. It has simply made it more complicated.

Mate selection in traditional societies may have revolved around health, resources, family, and social status. Modern society has added appearance, height, education, income, profession, personality, interests, values, communication skills, and lifestyle.

And once the internet appeared, many of these things could even be displayed directly. For example, through social media or dating apps.

In the past, you might meet someone first and gradually discover, “This person is actually pretty interesting.”

Now it might be the first photo, the second photo, height, occupation, a few Prompts, and then a left swipe or a right swipe.

In a sense, modern technology has turned natural selection into a user interface.

There is something deeply ironic about this.

On the one hand, we created civilization so that a person’s physical characteristics would no longer determine everything about them. On the other hand, we created an enormous marketplace in which all kinds of human characteristics could once again be compared.

So modern society may look increasingly civilized, but that does not necessarily mean we have truly escaped our animal nature.

Perhaps we have simply given our animal nature a more civilized package.

## **Postmodernity as a Refined Premodernity**

If you look closely at many aspects of modern human behavior, you will find that they are still surprisingly similar to those of premodern societies. The packaging has simply changed completely.

Primitive societies competed for food, territory, mates, and status within the group.

Modern societies compete for income, housing, careers, education, social circles, aesthetics, physical appearance, attention, and partners.

In the past, a person had to prove themselves within their tribe. Now a person can prove themselves on Instagram, WeChat Moments, LinkedIn, or dating apps.

Humans once judged a person’s value through physical characteristics and relationships within the group. Now we have invented countless numerical indicators: follower counts, likes, salaries, job titles, height, photos, educational credentials…

Even things like “what movies I like, what music I listen to, and what I do on weekends” can become part of a personal brand.

From this perspective, what we call “postmodernity” sometimes looks more like a more refined version of premodernity.

We have not completely escaped competition, desire, sex, status, or reproduction. We have simply made them more abstract and more efficient.

The most interesting part is that humans have even begun to treat this competition itself as something that can be reflected upon and criticized.

We discuss consumerism. We discuss alienation. We discuss appearance anxiety. We discuss the dating market. We discuss how algorithms influence our desires.

In other words, we are not only playing the game. We have also begun to observe ourselves and ask why we are playing it.

Perhaps this is what truly distinguishes humans from other animals.

Animals simply fight over things, while humans can sit down after the competition and ask:

**Why are we competing in the first place?**

And once that happens, the question is no longer limited to an individual’s life.

Because if a civilization can reflect on its own desires, then eventually it may even begin to reflect on its most fundamental desire.

Reproduction.

## **The Fermi Paradox and Silent Civilizations**

This leads me to an even larger question: if human civilization really becomes increasingly capable of escaping biological instincts as it develops, what about other civilizations?

This brings us back to the Fermi Paradox.

The universe is enormous, and there are an enormous number of stars. The possibility that life, or even technological civilizations, exists beyond Earth is not completely unimaginable. But if technological civilizations can arise many times, and if some of them eventually develop interstellar travel, automated probes, or even interstellar colonization, then according to some of the simplest lines of reasoning, the Milky Way should already bear abundant traces of them.

Yet so far, we have found no clear evidence of an extraterrestrial technological civilization.

And so we arrive at the famous question: **Where is everybody?**

The Fermi Paradox has countless possible explanations. Civilizations may be extremely rare. Technological civilizations may be very prone to destroying themselves. We may simply not have searched nearly enough. Extraterrestrial civilizations may use technologies that we cannot recognize. Or there may be some kind of “Great Filter” that makes one particular stage between the emergence of life and the development of an interstellar civilization extraordinarily difficult to pass.

But I suddenly found another explanation particularly interesting.

Perhaps we are making a very human-centric assumption: **We assume that the more advanced a civilization becomes, the more it will want to expand.**

And so the advanced civilizations we imagine are always extremely active: Dyson spheres, interstellar colonization, galactic empires, constantly occupying new star systems.

Because we assume that our own biological instincts work this way.

A living organism needs resources in order to survive. If it needs resources, it has to compete. Once there is competition, there is expansion. Once there is reproduction, there is constant replication.

So we naturally project this logic onto extraterrestrial civilizations.

But what if a civilization really becomes sufficiently advanced?

Suppose it has already solved disease, aging, energy, food, and survival.

Suppose it has extremely cheap energy, highly advanced artificial intelligence, completely immersive virtual worlds, and perhaps even the ability to create experiences within digital environments that far surpass anything the physical world can offer.

Then why would it still need to colonize the galaxy?

Why would it necessarily need more land?

Why would it necessarily need more people?

Why would it necessarily need to replicate itself?

Perhaps a primitive civilization has to expand because otherwise another civilization will consume it.

A modern nation still needs economic growth because population, resources, institutions, and competition all push it toward continued development.

But a truly advanced civilization might finally have the ability to ask the question that humanity is only slowly beginning to ask: **Why do I have to keep reproducing?**

If they eventually discovered that they could simply choose not to reproduce, things would become very interesting.

Because a civilization’s technological capabilities could continue to increase while its desire to expand continued to decline.

This means that our traditional image of an advanced civilization may have it exactly backwards.

**The more advanced a civilization becomes, the less it may resemble a galactic empire. It may instead resemble a civilization that has gradually lost its desire to expand.**

They have the ability to colonize the galaxy.

But they do not.

Not because they cannot.

But because there is no need.

Perhaps they are not even interested.

This leads me to a very absurd possibility:

Perhaps there have been countless civilizations in the universe.

They all went through the same progression, from life to intelligence, from intelligence to technology, and from technology to advanced civilization. They all eventually acquired the ability to expand into the universe.

And then they began reproducing less and less, competing less and less, needing fewer and fewer new resources, and spending more and more of their existence immersed in worlds they had created themselves.

In the end, they did not go extinct.

They simply became quiet.

And from the outside of the universe, it would look as though they had never existed at all.

This is, of course, only a thought experiment, not a scientific solution to the Fermi Paradox. The Fermi Paradox itself has no universally accepted solution, and the idea that “civilizations simply do not want to expand” is only one possibility among many.

But when I put it together with the population question from the beginning, it does leave me with a very strange thought.

Humanity has only just reached the stage where we possess enough ability to decide whether or not we reproduce, while still not having completely escaped reproduction itself.

So what happens if a civilization continues developing for ten thousand years, one hundred thousand years, or one million years?

Perhaps the real Great Filter is not nuclear war, nor the destruction of humanity by artificial intelligence, nor some cosmic catastrophe.

Perhaps it is civilization itself gradually acquiring enough intelligence to stop seeing “continuing to replicate ourselves” as something that goes without saying.

If that is the case, then perhaps the strangest thing about the Fermi Paradox is this:

**We keep searching for civilizations that have conquered the universe, without considering that a truly advanced civilization may no longer want to conquer anything at all.**

It may already possess the entire universe, and simply live quietly within it.

And perhaps the reason we have seen nothing is not that there has never been anyone out there.

Perhaps it is that the civilizations that once got ahead of us eventually became quiet.
